Thematic Exhibits: The Mechanics of Arbitrage

Explore the four historical milestones that dismantled the American worker's security over eighty years.

1945–1970 The Post-War Industrial Social Contract

The Treaty of Detroit & The Old Deal

In 1950, the United Auto Workers (UAW) and General Motors signed the historic “Treaty of Detroit.” In exchange for labor stability and a management monopoly over shop-floor discipline, workers received guaranteed annual wage increases tied to productivity, cost-of-living allowances (COLA), employer-paid healthcare, and defined-benefit pensions. For thirty years, this formula allowed a single manufacturing earner without higher education to own a suburban home, purchase an automobile, support a family, and guarantee generational upward mobility.

• The Industrial Multiplier Effect:
Direct manufacturing employment historically supported 3.5 to 5 indirect service, municipal, and retail jobs across rust-belt towns. When the foundry whistled, an entire town lived.
1970–2000 Shareholder Primacy & The Rusted Gate

The Rust Belt Requiem & The “Retraining” Fiction

In 1970, Milton Friedman declared that the sole responsibility of business is to maximize shareholder value. Within a decade, corporate raiding and leveraged buyouts decoupled wage growth from productivity gains. Between 1979 and 2020, American worker productivity grew by 61.8%, while wages increased by only 17.5%. When plants shuttered, the federal government offered Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA)—telling 50-year-old machinists to “Learn to Code.” In reality, these retraining initiatives operated as political placebos, blaming displaced workers for their own obsolescence while executives pocketed offshoring dividends.

• The Tuition Lifeboat Trap:
Industrial parents remortgaged homes to purchase university credentials for their children, hoping higher education would serve as an unsinkable lifeboat against manual labor displacement.
2000–2020 The Knowledge Worker Illusion

Digital Taylorism & The Knowledge Worker's Trap

Economist Robert Reich christened the college-educated workforce “Symbolic Analysts”—anointed professionals whose minds allegedly rendered them immune to deindustrialization. But inside glass office parks, management applied Frederick Winslow Taylor’s scientific management to cognitive labor. Through two-week Agile sprints, Jira ticket queues, and standardized documentation, idiosyncratic human expertise was codified into digital workflows. Simultaneously, Richard Baldwin’s “Globotics” enabled fiber-optic telemigration to Bangalore and Hyderabad, turning white-collar engineers into the architects of their own future replacement.

• The 401(k) Mirage:
Defined-benefit pensions were abolished in favor of volatile 401(k) plans and unvested stock options, shifting 100% of capital market risk directly onto individual workers.
2025–Present The Algorithmic Slaughter & The Final Solidarity

The White-Collar Recession & The Parking Lot Alliance

The white-collar collapse arrived not with physical security guards, but with automated calendar invitations and pre-recorded mass terminations. Drawing from real-world lawsuits (Disney, Southern California Edison), workers were forced to spend their final 30 days training cheaper foreign visa replacements or fine-tuning the very generative models replacing them to secure severance pay. Generative AI simultaneously eradicated the entry-level career rungs, stranding the next generation in debt. In the parking lot of the suburban office park, the displaced son and the discarded factory father finally recognize that blue- and white-collar were never different castes—they were merely successive cohorts on capital's ledger.

• The Broken Rung:
Junior developer, copywriter, and paralegal roles evaporated by 65% in 2024–2025, stranding graduates who paid \$150,000 for credentials with no entry point onto the economic ladder.
