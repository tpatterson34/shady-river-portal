God the Utterly Indifferent

Self-Authored Meaning

Philosophical Dossier • Being, Monism, and the Night Sky

Analyzing Trevor Swistchew’s synthesis of biblical ontology, Spinozan substance monism, and existential liberation

Deconstructing the External Clockmaker from Within

Swistchew performs a stunning textual maneuver: he takes the central pillars of Western scripture and shows that they do not support an external, anthropomorphic engineer. When the burning bush speaks in Exodus 3:14, the deity does not present a curriculum vitae of manufacture or a chronology of beginnings; it simply utters “Ehyeh Asher Ehyeh”—I AM THAT I AM. This is an ontological assertion of uncaused, self-subsisting presence. Furthermore, when Jesus declares in John 10:30 that “I and the Father are one,” the dualistic separation between humanity, divinity, and nature dissolves. There is no outside architect peering down from the rafters; there is only undivided being itself.

Deus sive Natura: The Reality of Raw Existence

Centuries after biblical texts were written, Baruch Spinoza recognized the same unavoidable truth: if substance is infinite, there can only be one substance (Deus sive Natura—God or Nature). Spinoza demonstrated that an uncaused universe does not require a divine carpenter shaping clay from beyond time. Modern quantum physics and cyclical cosmology arrive at an identical horizon: spacetime and energy do not demand a clockmaker with human vanity. Reality is not a product manufactured long ago in six literal days; it is an eternal, self-transforming presence expressing itself through stars, dust, biology, and human consciousness.

Wonder Without Fear: The Human Dignity of Meaning

Rather than descending into nihilism, Swistchew’s conclusion is profoundly humane and liberating. Believers often fear that without a cosmic supervisor, human life is rendered worthless. Swistchew turns this logic on its head: if a supervisor dictates purpose from above, humans are merely conscripted actors in an alien play. But in a vast, indifferent universe that owes us nothing, every drop of compassion, justice, art, and love we create is authentically ours. We are not small or accidental; we are the universe itself waking up to wonder at its own infinite, uncreated beauty.

Movement I

The Projection of Comfort & The Scriptural Paradox

How Humanity Flatters Itself with Cosmic Supervision

Human beings hold to the idea of a Creator because it flatters them. It gives a beginning, a parent, a cosmic supervisor who supposedly designed the universe with humanity in mind. It is a comforting story, and comfort is often mistaken for truth. Yet comfort has never been evidence, and belief has never been proof.

The universe shows no sign of having been created. No fingerprints. No blueprint. No moment of divine manufacture. What we see is existence raw, indifferent, uncaused. A reality that simply is, without asking permission and without offering explanation. The idea of a Creator is not a discovery; it is a projection. A story told by a frightened species staring into an infinite night.

What makes this even more striking is that the religious texts so often used to defend the Creator myth quietly dismantle it. In the Old Testament, the deity does not describe itself as a being with a beginning. It does not recount its own origin. It simply declares: I AM. A statement of existence without birth, without cause, without time. If taken seriously, this is not the voice of a cosmic engineer. It is the voice of existence itself—the same uncreated reality that physics now describes without invoking gods at all.

Then Jesus adds a line that should have ended the Creator debate forever: I and the Father are one. Not two beings. Not a hierarchy. Not a relationship. One. If the Father is the eternal I AM, and Jesus is one with that, then the boundary between “God” and “everything that exists” collapses instantly. There is no outside. No separate creator. No divine architect standing apart from the universe. There is only the universe itself—the totality, the whole, the uncreated.

Yet many believers refuse to see this even when it is written in their own scripture. They argue as if their identity depends on the argument being won. And in a sense, it does. The Creator myth gives them a narrative, a beginning, a sense of being watched and guided. To lose that is to face the truth that existence is indifferent, that meaning is not assigned from above, and that the universe owes them nothing.

Believers hold to the Creator idea like an extinct species refusing to accept its extinction. They defend it with passion, but passion is not evidence. They insist they are right, even when the logic collapses beneath them. They fight for a worldview that no longer fits the world.

But reality does not bend to belief. Existence does not require a creator. And the universe does not need a story to be real.

If God means anything at all, it is not a being but being itself—the uncreated, the indifferent, the eternal I AM that requires no beginning and offers no comfort. A universe that simply exists, without purpose, without plan, without apology.

And that truth, once seen, cannot be unseen.

Movement II

A Logical Examination: Uncaused Reality & The Extinct Worldview

The Collapse of Machine Theology & The Dawn of Authentic Agency

Many believers refuse to accept this, even when it is written in their own scripture. They argue with the desperation of a species already extinct but unwilling to acknowledge it. They defend: a literal timeline, a literal creation event, a literal anthropomorphic deity, and a universe built like a machine. But these ideas no longer fit the world. They no longer fit the evidence. They no longer fit their own text. What they defend is not truth—it is identity.

The universe does not need a creator to exist. Existence does not require a starting point. Reality does not need a story to be real. If the word “God” means anything at all, it is not a being but being itself—the uncreated, indifferent, eternal I AM that simply exists without purpose, without plan, without apology. This is not atheism. This is not mysticism. This is the logical consequence of taking existence seriously.

The universe is not a parent. It is not a judge. It is not a storyteller. It is indifferent—utterly, perfectly indifferent. And yet, in that indifference lies a strange liberation. Meaning is not assigned from above. Purpose is not dictated. Life is not supervised. We exist in a universe that owes us nothing—and therefore, everything we build, discover, and understand is genuinely ours.

Once seen, this truth cannot be unseen: The Creator idea is unnecessary. The universe is uncreated. Existence simply is. And “God,” if the word is to survive at all, can only mean the totality of being—not a separate being who made it.

Movement III

A Story for Those Who Seek a Wider View

The Gentle Liberation of Wonder, Presence & Self-Authored Meaning

Most people inherit their beliefs long before they ever examine them. The story of a loving God creating the world is comforting, and because it is comforting, it becomes precious. Years later, when life becomes complicated, that early story still sits in the background offering reassurance even when the adult no longer remembers where it came from. This is not a flaw. It is simply human.

But there comes a moment for some people when the old story no longer answers the questions they carry. They begin to sense that the universe is larger, older, stranger, and more beautiful than the explanations they were given. They feel a quiet pull toward something deeper. Not rebellion. Not anger. Just curiosity. This chapter is for those people.

Imagine someone sitting alone one evening, looking up at the night sky. They are not trying to prove anything. They are not trying to win an argument. They are simply wondering: “How did all this begin?” It is a gentle question, but it opens a door.

If they look honestly, they notice something surprising: the universe does not behave like something that was built. It behaves like something that simply is—vast, continuous, without edges or seams. And for the first time, they consider the possibility that existence might not need a beginning at all. This thought does not frighten them. It frees them.

Modern cosmology, philosophy, and even mystical traditions converge on a simple insight: Existence does not require a starting point. The universe may not have been created in the way a potter shapes clay or a carpenter builds a house. It may be eternal, cyclical, or part of a reality far beyond human imagination. This does not diminish wonder. It expands it.

Instead of a universe made once, long ago, we find a universe that is alive in every moment—unfolding, transforming, expressing itself through stars, planets, life, and consciousness. A universe that is not a product, but a presence.

A person does not need to abandon their faith to explore this wider view. They only need to loosen their grip on the idea that truth must fit inside a single story. Belief can grow. Understanding can deepen. Perspective can widen. And none of this requires rejecting the comfort or meaning that faith once provided. It simply means allowing the universe to be as vast as it truly is.

By the end of this journey, the reader discovers something valuable: They are not small. They are not accidental. They are not dependent on a single ancient narrative to feel grounded. They are part of a universe that is continuous, eternal, and profoundly interconnected. Whether they call that universe “God,” “creation,” “nature,” or simply “reality” is entirely their choice. What matters is that they see themselves as part of something larger—not because someone told them to believe it, but because they felt it for themselves.

The purpose of this chapter is not to win an argument. It is to open a window. To show that belief does not have to be rigid. That faith does not have to be fearful. That understanding does not have to be inherited—it can be discovered. And that the universe, indifferent though it may be, is still a place where meaning can be found, created, and lived. Not because someone designed it that way, but because we are capable of finding meaning within it.
