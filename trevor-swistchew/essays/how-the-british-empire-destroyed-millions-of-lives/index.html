How the British Empire Destroyed Millions of Lives

Names, Roles, and the Structural Complicity of Imperial Extraction

TS
Trevor Swistchew
Essayist, Philosopher & Historian • Western Washington / Scotland
“The British Empire was not a civilising mission. It was a system that repeatedly chose power and profit over human life... That is how millions of lives were methodically destroyed.” — Trevor Swistchew

The Forensic Ledger of Imperial Atrocity & Complicity

Archival Research & Thematic Analysis

Bengal Famine (1770) & 1943 Famine

Over 10 million starved in 1770 while East India Company grain monopolies extracted maximum tax revenue. In 1943, 3 million Bengalis perished while Winston Churchill diverted food reserves to Mediterranean war stockpiles.

Amritsar Massacre (1919)

General Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire on unarmed Sikh, Hindu, and Muslim celebrants at Jallianwala Bagh, killing up to 1,500 people.

Kenya: Mau Mau Camps (1950s)

Over 100,000 Kikuyu detained in brutal concentration camps with systematic torture, followed by the covert destruction of imperial files at Hanslope Park (Operation Legacy).

Ireland: The Great Hunger (1845–1852)

Over 1 million starved while food (wheat, oats, cattle) was continuously exported from Irish ports under armed British military escort to protect market profits.

The Slavery Compensation Act of 1837

The British state borrowed £20 million (£17 billion modern equivalent)—amounting to 40% of the national budget—to compensate 46,000 slave-owners for the loss of human property.

Enslaved human beings received £0 in compensation, forced into another 4 to 6 years of unpaid "apprenticeship."

Section 01

NAMES, ROLES, COMPLICITY.

The British Empire was not a civilising mission. It was a system that repeatedly chose power and profit over human life and it did so through decisions made by identifiable people in government, the military, and business. This is a direct, factual expose of how those decisions wrecked millions of lives.

The pattern is simple and brutal: famine used as policy, camps and torture used as tools of rule, massacres justified as “order,” slavery embedded in law and then compensated for its owners, and records destroyed to hide the worst of it. Violence was not a mistake; it was built into the machinery of empire.

India — Famine and Massacre as Instruments of Rule

Edward Robert Bulwer-Lytton — Viceroy of India (1876–1880): During the Great Famine of 1876–78, Lytton enforced a rigid “free market” line. Grain exports continued, relief was deliberately limited, and millions starved. He chose to prioritise imperial fiscal discipline and trade over keeping people alive. His policy decisions helped turn drought into mass death.

Winston Churchill — Prime Minister (1940–1945), former Colonial Secretary: In the Bengal famine of 1943, around 3 million people died. Churchill’s War Cabinet controlled shipping and food policy; repeated requests for grain imports to India were refused while food and shipping were directed elsewhere. Those decisions, taken at the top, turned a crisis into a catastrophe. Earlier, as Colonial Secretary, Churchill defended General Reginald Dyer after the Amritsar massacre.

Reginald Dyer — Brigadier General, Amritsar (1919): Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed crowd trapped in Jallianwala Bagh until ammunition ran out. Hundreds were killed, many more wounded. This was a deliberate act of terror to “teach a lesson” to colonial subjects carried out under the authority of the British state.

Africa — Camps, Torture, and Deliberate Cover-up

Herbert Kitchener — Commander-in-Chief, Boer War (1899–1902): Kitchener oversaw the British campaign in South Africa, including the creation of concentration camps for Boer civilians and Black Africans. Tens of thousands died from disease and malnutrition in these camps. Targeting families and communities was part of a strategy to break resistance.

Clement Attlee — Prime Minister (1945–1951): Attlee’s government ran colonial emergencies in Malaya and began the detention regime in Kenya. Emergency regulations allowed detention without trial, forced resettlement, and collective punishment. His cabinet maintained the legal framework that made mass coercion routine.

Harold Macmillan — Prime Minister (1957–1963): Under Macmillan, the Mau Mau Emergency in Kenya reached its peak in terms of detention and torture. Colonial authorities ran camps and “screening” centres where beatings, sexual assault, and forced labour were systematically used. As decolonisation approached, officials destroyed or removed thousands of files in what became known as Operation Legacy, deliberately hiding evidence of abuse.

Ireland — Famine and Reprisals Under British Rule

British Cabinets under Sir Robert Peel, Lord John Russell and others — Great Famine (1845–1852): During the Irish famine, food exports continued while the population starved. Relief was limited, and ideological commitment to market principles and property rights outweighed the urgency of mass starvation. Policy choices turned crop failure into a national catastrophe.

David Lloyd George’s government and military leadership — War of Independence (1919–1921): Under Lloyd George, forces such as the Black and Tans and Auxiliaries carried out reprisals in Ireland: burning homes, beating civilians, and committing extrajudicial killings. These actions were part of a state strategy to crush resistance, not random violence.

Section 02

Slavery and Compensation — Law Built on Human Ownership

Charles II — King (1660–1685): Charles II granted the Royal African Company its charter, enabling large-scale trafficking of enslaved Africans. This embedded slavery into the English and later British state and economy.

Parliament and Cabinet of the 1830s — Slavery Abolition Act (1833): When slavery was abolished, the British state chose to compensate slave-owners, not the enslaved. The Slave Compensation Commission paid out vast sums to owners across Britain, including many in Scotland and England. The decision entrenched slavery’s profits into British wealth while leaving the victims with nothing.

Section 03

Monarchs as Legitimisers of Imperial Power

Elizabeth I authorised early slave-trading voyages and colonial ventures. Queen Victoria reigned over the high tide of imperial expansion including famines and conquests. George V was monarch during Amritsar and the Irish War of Independence. Elizabeth II reigned during the Mau Mau Emergency and the later legal reckoning that exposed Britain’s torture system.

They did not draft every order or design every camp, but their reigns and signatures gave constitutional and symbolic cover to the governments and officials who did.

Section 04

The Core of Complicity

None of these people acted alone. Their complicity lies in the choices they made and the systems they upheld.

They enforced famine policies that let millions starve rather than disrupt markets. They authorised and defended camps, torture, and collective punishment as tools of “order.” They compensated slave-owners instead of victims. They allowed reprisals and massacres to be justified as necessary discipline. They destroyed or hid records to avoid future accountability.

That is how millions of lives were methodically destroyed: not by one single villain, but by a network of power that treated human beings as expendable in the service of empire.
