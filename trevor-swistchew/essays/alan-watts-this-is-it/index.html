Alan Watts: This Is It

On the Myth of Separation, the Cosmic Dance, and the Ultimate Chord of the Present

  1. 6th Century BCE — The Tao & The River of Becoming

    Lao Tzu (*Tao Te Ching*) and Heraclitus articulate that reality is perpetual flux (*panta rhei*). Trying to grasp the moment is like trying to catch water in a sieve.

  2. 8th Century CE — Advaita Vedanta & Non-Duality

    Adi Shankara synthesizes the Upanishadic revelation: the individual soul (*Atman*) and the ultimate ground of existence (*Brahman*) are identical. The illusion of separation is *Maya*.

  3. 1958–1960 — Alan Watts & "This Is It"

    Alan Watts publishes his breakthrough essay and book *This Is It*, introducing Zen and non-dual spontaneity to Western thinkers, dissolving spiritual ambition into playful immediacy.

  4. 2026 — Trevor Swistchew & The Shady River Bard

    Trevor Swistchew crystallizes the philosophy into prose, inspiring The Shady River Bard to adapt the essay into an explosive, heavy-rock spoken-word musical experience.

Tathātā (Suchness / Thisness)

In Mahayana Buddhism and Zen, *tathātā* represents the world perceived directly as it is, stripped of labels, anxieties, conceptual narratives, and self-serving interpretations.

Līlā (The Cosmic Play)

The Hindu principle that existence is not a grim moral trial or assembly line, but spontaneous divine improvisation—a game where God plays hide-and-seek with itself through every living creature.

The Skin-Encapsulated Ego

Watts' diagnosis of modern neurosis: the tragic hallucination that you are a stranger stranded inside a body, rather than an organic outgrowth of the physical earth.

The Ultimate Chord

The underlying resonance of being. Not an esoteric frequency reserved for ascetics, but the quiet background hum of existence echoing through breath, water, and consciousness.

I. The Sacred in the Ordinary

Alan Watts had a way of saying things that made the floor drop out from under you1. Not with force, not with argument, not with the heavy machinery of philosophy, but with a kind of playful clarity that left you staring at the world as if you had never seen it before. He didn’t try to convince you of anything. He simply pointed, gently, as if to say, “Look. You already know.” And the strange thing is that he was right. You did already know. You had just forgotten.

Watts understood something most people never grasp: that the sacred is not elsewhere. It is not hidden behind the stars, not waiting in some distant heaven, not locked behind rituals or doctrines or moral tests2. It is here. It is now. It is this. The ordinary moment, the breath you are taking, the sensation of being alive in a body you did not choose — this is the thing people spend their entire lives searching for. They look for meaning in the future, in the afterlife, in the next achievement, in the next relationship, in the next spiritual trick. But the truth is embarrassingly simple. There is no next. There is only this.

"There is no next. There is only this. This moment — the one you are in right now — is the only moment that has ever existed."
II. The Illusion of the Corridor

Watts called it “This Is It,” and he meant it literally3. Not as a metaphor, not as a slogan, but as the most direct statement of reality a human being can make. This moment — the one you are in right now — is the only moment that has ever existed. The past is memory. The future is imagination. The present is the only place where life actually happens. But humans are experts at missing it. They live in stories about what was or what might be, and they treat the present as an inconvenient corridor between two illusions.

The tragedy is that people think they are chasing life, when in fact they are running from it. They chase goals, identities, beliefs, spiritual experiences, anything that promises to deliver them to some final state of completion. But completion never arrives. It cannot arrive, because the seeker is the thing being sought. The one who is searching is already the thing they hope to find4. Watts understood this, and he spent his life trying to remind people of what they already were.

III. The Dance of the Cosmos

He would say that the universe is not a machine but a dance5. Not a problem to be solved but a performance to be experienced. Not a test but a play. And you are not a separate observer watching from the outside. You are part of the dance, part of the performance, part of the universe expressing itself in human form. You are not in the world. You are the world, temporarily appearing as a person. The wave is not separate from the ocean. The wave is the ocean, moving in a particular shape for a brief moment before returning to itself.

"You are not in the world. You are the world, temporarily appearing as a person. The wave is not separate from the ocean. The wave is the ocean, moving in a particular shape for a brief moment before returning to itself."
IV. The Cosmic Joke & The Ultimate Chord

This is why Watts laughed so much. He saw the cosmic joke. He saw that humans spend their lives pretending to be separate, pretending to be isolated egos trapped inside fragile bodies, pretending to be strangers in a universe that is, in truth, their own deeper self. He saw that the fear, the anxiety, the guilt, the shame, the endless striving — all of it comes from forgetting what you are. You are not a visitor here. You are not an outsider. You are not a mistake. You are the universe looking at itself through a pair of human eyes6.

When Watts said “This Is It,” he was not offering comfort. He was offering liberation. He was saying that nothing is missing. Nothing is elsewhere. Nothing is withheld. The meaning people search for is not hidden. It is simply overlooked. It is in the breath, the heartbeat, the sensation of being aware. It is in the ordinary moment that people dismiss as unimportant. It is in the silence between thoughts. It is in the simple fact of existence.

The Ultimate Chord, the deep vibration beneath everything, is not a secret frequency you must earn. It is the hum of being itself. It is the sound of existence happening. It is the recognition that you are not separate from the universe but an expression of it. Watts understood this chord. He heard it in the laughter of children, in the movement of water, in the rise and fall of breath, in the absurdity of human seriousness. He heard it everywhere because it is everywhere.

V. The Ground Beneath Your Feet

And once you hear it, once you truly hear it, the world changes. Not because the world has changed, but because you have stopped looking through the lens of separation. You stop trying to escape the moment. You stop trying to become something else. You stop trying to reach a future that never arrives. You begin to see that the only thing you were ever running from was the present, and the present was the only place you could ever be free.

Watts did not give answers. He removed the need for them. He did not offer salvation. He revealed that there was nothing to be saved from. He did not point to a path. He pointed to the ground beneath your feet. He did not promise enlightenment. He simply reminded you that you are already here.

"Watts did not give answers. He removed the need for them. He did not point to a path. He pointed to the ground beneath your feet. This is it. It always was. It always will be."

This is it. It always was. It always will be.

Creative Genesis & Liner Notes

Most musical treatments of Alan Watts adopt gentle ambient synthesizers, meditative bells, or low-fi chillhop beats. The Shady River Bard took a radically opposite direction: meeting the existential shock of Watts' message with the thunderous visceral power of heavy rock and metal.

When Watts states that "the floor drops out from under you," that is not a gentle stroll—it is a cosmic earthquake. The heavy, overdriven riffs and crushing percussion echo the tearing away of dualistic illusions, while the spoken word delivery drives home Trevor Swistchew's poignant phrasing with unrelenting force.

The result is an electrifying collision of Eastern enlightenment and modern rock rebellion: a sonic reminder that awakening is not passive, but a roaring celebration of the present.

  4. [4] The paradox of the seeker: in the words of 13th-century Sufi poet Rumi, "What you seek is seeking you," or in the Zen metaphor, searching for your spectacles while they are sitting on your nose. ↩
  5. [5] Watts frequently compared life to music and dance rather than a business journey: "In music one doesn't make the end of a composition the point of the composition; if so, the best conductors would be those who played fastest." ↩
  6. [6] See Alan Watts, The Book: On the Taboo Against Knowing Who You Are (Pantheon Books, 1966), detailing the dissolution of the "skin-encapsulated ego." ↩
