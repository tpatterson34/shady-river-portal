The Silver of Skullyville

March 23, 1847 (Skullyville, Choctaw Nation to County Cork & Doolough Pass, Ireland): Sixteen Winters After the Trail of Tears, $170 in Tarnished Iskuli Silver, and the 173-Year Circle of Grace

“On March 23, 1847, inside a rain-lashed log council agency at Skullyville in the Choctaw Nation (from the Choctaw word iskuli, meaning 'silver coin'), families who had survived the brutal 1831 Trail of Tears blizzard across the Mississippi just sixteen winters earlier gathered by whale-oil lantern light to hear a famine relief circular from across the Atlantic. In Ireland, Black '47 was turning potato fields black while guarded grain ships sailed out of Irish harbors. Having almost nothing left on earth themselves, the Choctaw people untied knotted linen rags and pooled $170 in tarnished silver—routing it via Memphis and New York to Quaker soup cauldrons in starving Irish parishes. One hundred and seventy years later, nine twenty-foot steel eagle feathers rose in County Cork in the shape of an empty bowl—and when pandemic sickness struck Native nations in 2020, thousands of Irish families sent millions back across the sea in $17 and $170 gifts.”

— Historical & Moral Thesis • Single #01 of International Singles Volume 1 (1831 – March 23, 1847 – May 2020)

The 1831 Memphis Blizzard & Ireland's Black '47 Export Quays

Why did Choctaw elders in 1847 immediately recognize the ache of Irish starvation? Because sixteen winters earlier they had buried their own kin in the frozen mud between Mississippi and Indian Territory.

Witnessed by Alexis de Tocqueville at the icy Mississippi River crossing in Memphis in December 1831, the Choctaw Nation was the first Native nation forced down the Trail of Tears. Across the ocean in 1847, British laissez-faire policy under Sir Charles Trevelyan shut down relief depots while armed redcoats guarded ships of oats, wheat, and butter sailing out of Cork, Waterford, and Limerick.

“No elder there had need to ask / How hunger cuts into the bone / They still could feel the Memphis snow / Where half their frozen kin had gone”

$170 vs. 600
Verified April 3, 1847 in the Arkansas Intelligencer • Routed via Memphis & NY to Quaker soup kettles

March 23, 1847 ($170 in Iskuli) vs. Doolough Pass (600 in Sleet)

At Skullyville (from Choctaw 'iskuli' = silver coin), impoverished Choctaw families pooled $170 for Quaker relief ships—contrasting with Poor Law inspectors at Delphi Lodge turning 600 starving neighbors into sleet.

Recorded contemporaneously in the Arkansas Intelligencer ('The whole amount subscribed was $170! Truly, the poor help the poor'), the gift bought sacks of Indian meal distributed by non-sectarian Quakers in Cork and Mayo—while on March 30–31, 1849, inspectors Hogrove and Primrose forced 600 starving people to walk 12 miles along Doolough Pass in freezing sleet only to deny them bread.

“While up on dark Doolough's pass / Six hundred walked the freezing sleet / Turned back from Delphi's lighted lodge / Yet strangers sent us bread to eat!”
PILLAR III // THE UNBROKEN CIRCLE
173 Winters
Nine 20-ft steel eagle feathers in Midleton, Cork (2017) • Thousands of $17 & $170 Irish gifts in 2020

Doolough (1990), 'Kindred Spirits' (2017) & The $2.6M Return Gift (2020)

Empires decay, but a debt of grace between wounded peoples never dies: from the 1990–1992 walks and Midleton's nine steel feathers to Ireland's 2020 pandemic relief to the Navajo and Hopi Nations.

In June 2017, sculptor Alex Pentek and Choctaw Chief Gary Batton unveiled 'Kindred Spirits' in Bailick Park, Midleton—nine 20-foot stainless-steel eagle feathers forming an empty famine bowl. Three years later in May 2020, ordinary Irish families sent over $2.6M to COVID-stricken Native reservations in $17 and $170 increments: 'From Ireland, 173 years later.'

“Nine steel feathers touch the skies / Like an empty bowl that never dies! / A hundred seventy winters turned ... And Ireland sent the millions home”

The Bard’s Story • Complete Narrative Essay

History books are filled with treaties signed by kings, loans negotiated by imperial banks, and charity dispensed from marble palaces by empires trying to burnish their image. 'The Silver of Skullyville'—launching International Singles Volume 1—tells the exact opposite of imperial charity. In the early spring of 1847, during the darkest winter of the Irish Great Famine (An Gorta Mór, 'Black '47'), word of starving Irish families dying beside cold turf hearths while guarded ships of oats and beef sailed out of County Cork harbors crossed four thousand miles of ocean and reached a frontier log agency town on the Arkansas River in Indian Territory called Skullyville.

The people gathered inside that timber hall were Choctaws. Just sixteen winters earlier (1831–1833), following the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, the United States government had uprooted 15,000 Choctaws from ancestral Mississippi and marched them barefoot into the ferocious December 1831 Memphis blizzard—witnessed firsthand by Alexis de Tocqueville and described by Choctaw Chief Harkins as 'a trail of tears and death.' Between 2,500 and 6,000 Choctaws died along that frozen road. Even the name 'Skullyville' came from the Choctaw word 'iskuli-felah' ('iskuli' meaning silver coin, where meager treaty annuities were paid). Yet on March 23, 1847, when Indian Agent Major William Armstrong read the Memphis Irish Relief circular aloud by whale-oil flame, Choctaw elders, families, traders, and missionaries untied knotted linen rags and pooled $170 in silver (verified in the Arkansas Intelligencer on April 3, 1847: 'Truly, the poor help the poor').

Routed downriver through Memphis and New York to the Society of Friends (Quakers), that Choctaw iskuli bought sacks of American Indian cornmeal for non-sectarian Quaker soup cauldrons across County Cork and western Ireland—standing in searing moral contrast to the March 30–31, 1849 Doolough Pass tragedy in County Mayo, where two well-fed Poor Law inspectors at Delphi Lodge ordered 600 starving parishioners to walk twelve miles overnight through howling sleet, only to turn them back empty-handed at dawn.

One hundred and seventy winters later, that sacred bond came full circle: in 1990 and 1992 Choctaw and Irish walkers retraced Doolough Pass and 500 miles of the Trail of Tears; in June 2017 Midleton, County Cork unveiled Alex Pentek's 'Kindred Spirits'—nine twenty-foot stainless-steel eagle feathers forming an empty famine bowl reaching skyward; and in May 2020, when COVID-19 swept the Navajo and Hopi Nations, tens of thousands of Irish families sent over $2.6 million across the ocean in exact $17 and $170 increments, writing: 'From Ireland, 173 years later. The debt of grace is never forgotten.'

Dedicated to the Choctaw Nation elders and families of Skullyville (March 23, 1847), the starving parishioners of County Cork and Doolough Pass (1847–1849), the Society of Friends, and the thousands of Irish citizens who honored a 173-year debt of grace in 2020.
