Interactive Framework I // 3-Pillar Historical Matrix

The Kinship, The Rigged Inveraray Jury & The Psalm in the Sleet (1752–1755)

Every verse of 'James Stewart of the Glens (Seumas a' Ghlinne)' is anchored in the 1752 Inveraray trial record, James's verbatim November 8, 1752 scaffold speech, and Appin parish archives—avoiding sectarianism to indict institutional courtroom corruption.

PILLAR I // THE KINSHIP & THE TRAP (MAY 1752)
May 14, 1752
Appin Stewarts sheltered Chief Lachlan MacLachlan's household • Whitsunday eviction writs

From Loch Fyne's Widows to the Shot in Lettermore Wood

Directly linked to 'The Guns of Loch Fyne'—Chief Lachlan MacLachlan's wife was Mary Stewart of Appin—50-year-old farmer James Stewart cared for war widows and fought tenant evictions peacefully through Edinburgh courts until his legal Bill of Suspension was quashed.

On May 14, 1752, Crown factor Colin Roy Campbell of Glenure ('The Red Fox') rode south from Ballachulish ferry toward Duror carrying writs to evict five loyal Stewart tenant families on Whitsunday. Struck from behind by two musket balls fired from Lettermore birchwood while Alan Breck Stewart slipped into the mist, Glenure bled to death on the road—handing Hanoverian prosecutors the pretext they wanted.

“I kept a roof in Glen Duror for the widows of the war / For MacLachlan's grieving household when they fled Loch Fyne's dark shore”
PILLAR II // INSTITUTIONAL CORRUPTION (SEPT 1752)
11 of 15 Jurors
3rd Duke of Argyll on the bench • 4 months incommunicado at Fort William • 50-hr nonstop trial

Eleven of Fifteen on the Jury & Fifty Continuous Sleepless Hours

Despite dozens of witnesses proving James never left his farm in Glen Duror on May 14, Crown officials locked him incommunicado in Fort William for four months and moved his trial into Clan Campbell headquarters at Inveraray.

Presiding on the crimson velvet bench as Lord Justice General was Archibald Campbell, 3rd Duke of Argyll, overlooking a 15-man jury packed with 11 Campbells. Rather than expose the young hotbloods of Appin (including marksman Donald Stewart, whom kinsmen had to physically tie down to stop him from confessing) and trigger a military burning of every roof in the glen, James kept his peace as the 50-hour trial ground to a foregone conviction 'in airts and pairts.'

“Eleven out of fifteen men all bore one chieftain's crest / I knew if I named the hotblood boys, they'd burn out every nest”
PILLAR III // THE PSALM, THE WIRE & THE OUTCAST (1752–1755)
Nov 8, 1752 – 1755
Psalm 35 sung in Gaelic in a sleet gale • Wired skeleton felled by 'Daft' MacPhee • Buried at Keil

Salm Seumas a' Ghlinne (Psalm 35), Daft MacPhee & Old Keil Churchyard

Hanged in a howling November sleet storm above the Ballachulish narrows after singing Psalm 35 in Scottish Gaelic, James's remains were kept wired together inside an iron gibbet cage for years as a warning to every passing ferry.

Whenever winter gales shook the bleached joints loose, garrison soldiers were ordered up ladders to twist wire through shoulder and rib. When strong Highland men were too terrified of the gallows law to touch the timber, a gentle parish outcast called 'Daft' MacPhee climbed the knoll in a midnight tempest, tore the rotted gibbet into Loch Leven, and allowed James's wife Margaret to wrap what fell in clean white linen and bury him at ruined Keil Churchyard.

“Strong Highland men grew terrified to touch that cursed tree / So it took a gentle simple soul the parish called MacPhee”

The Bard’s Story • Complete Narrative Essay

Six years after the cannon smoke cleared from Culloden Moor and the shores of Loch Fyne, a single musket ball fired from the birch and hazel trees of Lettermore Wood on May 14, 1752 gave Scotland its most notorious judicial murder. When our Scottish friend and contributor Bob McNair (@bobmcnair9234)—whose ancestral family records inspired 'The Guns of Loch Fyne'—suggested the Appin Murder, he pointed out a hidden family thread linking the two stories directly together: Lachlan MacLachlan, 17th Chief of Clan MacLachlan who fell at Culloden, was married to Mary Stewart of Appin. When Castle Lachlan was abandoned on Loch Fyne, it was the Appin Stewarts—the household and kin of James Stewart of the Glens (Seumas a' Ghlinne)—who gave shelter to widowed families of the Rising.

On May 14, 1752, Crown factor Colin Roy Campbell of Glenure ('The Red Fox') rode toward Appin carrying Whitsunday eviction writs to clear loyal Stewart tenants off forfeited Ardsheal lands. Shot in the back from the hillside trees, he died on the road while the unseen marksman vanished into the mist and James's volatile foster-son, Alan Breck Stewart, slipped away toward France. Hanoverian London demanded swift political terror. Even though 50-year-old farmer James Stewart was miles away working peacefully at his farm in Glen Duror with an ironclad alibi, troops locked him in Fort William incommunicado for four months—barring his lawyers and wife until the eve of trial. Marched to Inveraray, the stronghold of the dead man's clan, James stood before Archibald Campbell, 3rd Duke of Argyll, presiding on the velvet bench as Lord Justice General, facing a fifteen-man jury packed with eleven Campbells. After sitting for fifty continuous hours without sleep, the court convicted James 'in airts and pairts' (as an accessory) so the Crown would have its example.

Why didn't an educated man fluent in both Gaelic and English tear the prosecution apart or point toward the hot-blooded young conspirators in the parish? Because James of the Glens knew Hanoverian military policy in his bones: if he exposed a clan plot without handing over a fugitive, King George's dragoons would burn every thatched roof in Appin to the ground. He offered one quiet neck upon the hill to save the roofs of all. On November 8, 1752, marched through a howling sleet gale to the rocky knoll (Cnap a' Chaolais) overlooking the Ballachulish ferry narrows, James read his calm written declaration of complete innocence and sang Psalm 35 in Scottish Gaelic ('False witnesses did rise up; they laid to my charge things that I know not')—singing with such unearthly dignity that the Redcoat guards stared down at their boots in shame and grief.

Even death did not satisfy London's desire to terrorize the Highlands. James's body was hung in iron chains on the hilltop gibbet above the Ballachulish narrows for eighteen months to nearly three years, guarded by soldiers who wired his bleached shoulder and rib bones back together whenever winter storms shook them loose—forcing every mother and child crossing the ferry to look up in silence. Strong Highland men were too terrified of the gallows law to touch that cursed timber. Finally, on a roaring black night of Highland gale, a gentle, simple-minded parish outcast remembered in folklore as 'Daft' MacPhee climbed the hill alone, wrenched the rotted gibbet out of the earth, and hurled it into Loch Leven. Gathering the bones that washed and fell onto the stones in clean white linen, James's faithful wife Margaret laid him quietly beneath the grass at ruined Keil Churchyard by the Appin sea—where the Duke's court is long forgotten dust, and Salm Seumas a' Ghlinne is still sung.

Dedicated to the memory of James Stewart of the Glens (Seumas a' Ghlinne, d. November 8, 1752), his faithful wife Margaret Cameron Stewart, parish outcast MacPhee, and our Scottish friend and historian Bob McNair (@bobmcnair9234) for shining light into the glens.
